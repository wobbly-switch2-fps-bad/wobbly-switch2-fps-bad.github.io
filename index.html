The recordings are 30 fps captures. The numbers below are the frames the game actually rendered, counted as unique frames in each capture. The two split-screen views always update on the same frames.

How the numbers were derived

1. Find the new frames

The recorder saves up to 30 frames per second. When the game is slower, the same image gets saved twice. Each frame is compared with the one before it. It counts as a new game frame if more than 0.2% of its pixels changed. Repeated frames show exactly 0.00% change, so they're easy to tell apart.

2. FPS avg

New frames ÷ clip length.

Clip 3: 790 frames recorded, 613 new, over 30.0 s → 20.4 fps

3. Worst 1s

A 1-second window slides along the clip in 0.25 s steps, counting new frames at each position. The lowest count is the worst second.

Clip 9: lowest 1-second window has 15 new frames → 15 fps

Check it yourself

Download any clip and run ffmpeg's built-in duplicate-frame filter. The final frame= count is the number of new frames. Divide it by the clip length:

ffmpeg -i clip3.mp4 -an -vf mpdecimate -fps_mode vfr -f null -

This independent method gives 611 frames for clip 3 (vs 613), 28.0 fps for clip 1 (vs 28.2), and 18.5 fps for clip 9 (vs 18.5).

Caveats: the recorder tops out at 30 fps and sometimes missed capture slots (up to ~20% in clip 9), so treat these numbers as a floor. The game may have run slightly faster in clips 3, 4, 5 and 9. The videos are the original recordings, repackaged from .mov to .mp4 without re-encoding.
